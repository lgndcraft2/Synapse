Why We Forget What We Read, and What the Research on Memory Consolidation Suggests About Changing It

It is a familiar experience to reach the end of a long article and find that very little of it remains, a phenomenon that researchers in cognitive psychology have approached from several directions, including the way working memory limits how much new information can be held at once, the role that sleep appears to play in consolidating what was encountered during the day, and the observation, repeated across many studies, that rereading a passage tends to produce a feeling of familiarity that is easily mistaken for understanding, whereas actively attempting to recall the material without looking at it, a technique often described as retrieval practice, tends to strengthen the memory itself even when the attempt is effortful or partly unsuccessful.

Spacing those attempts out over days rather than concentrating them in a single sitting appears to amplify the effect, although the mechanisms behind this spacing effect remain debated, with some accounts emphasising the additional effort required to retrieve partly forgotten material and others pointing to the variety of contexts in which the material ends up being encountered, and practical recommendations drawn from this literature therefore tend to combine short self-tests with deliberately delayed review.

None of this means that reading is wasted effort; rather, it suggests that the experience of reading fluently is a poor guide to what will be remembered, and that small changes in how a reader engages with a text after the first pass may matter more than the speed or completeness of that first pass.
